## 3. Shortlist (most useful for THIS project)

1. **B04 CADD: Chen, Shin & Dadras, RAID 2024 (project source [8]).**
   - Why it matters. This is the closest peer-reviewed analogue of detector D1 on longitudinal dynamics (R2). It uses context estimates (road grade, tire slippage, total mass) to cross-validate groups of production-vehicle data instead of "predict-input-then-compare". It answers RQ3: a physics-only detector competing with learned baselines. The full text compares against control-invariant baselines, including an LSTM-based one (keyword search only, not read in detail). It also answers RQ4: the smallest deviation it can localise is 0.07 g.
   - Key numbers (abstract): ">87,000 test-cases"; ">96% recall and <0.5% false positive rate"; ">95% accuracy when the vehicle's behavior deviates 0.07g (0.69 m/s2)".
   - Data: 13 real-world driving traces collected with OpenXC VI plus CarSim (full-text keyword search). The vehicle make was not checked.
2. **B03 SAID: Xue et al., USENIX Security 2022 (project source [7]).**
   - Why it matters. It uses explicit vehicle dynamics models: roll dynamics, steering/yaw dynamics and accelerating/braking dynamics (full text, sec. 4). These are a template for R4 and R6 and for writing relations as state-layer rules. Its threat model differs from ours: it filters injected commands at the entry point rather than detecting tampered sensor signals. This is relevant to RQ1 and RQ4 framing.
   - Key numbers (abstract): "defend against more than 99% of the network and service layer attack traffic and all state layer MIAs"; evaluated with "both real road data and simulation data". Code and datasets are public (GitHub rewhy/said).
3. **B06 van Wyk et al., IEEE T-ITS 2020.**
   - Why it matters. It is the most-cited record here (304, S2, 2026-11-23). It is also the strongest published evidence on RQ1 and RQ2: a hybrid of a physics/model residual (Kalman filter with chi-square) and a learned detector (CNN) beats each component alone. The project can cite it as precedent for "physics as extra inputs / companion to a learned detector".
   - Key claim (abstract): the combined approach "outperforms the anomaly detection and identification capabilities of both CNNs and Kalman filtering with a chi-square detector method alone". The abstract gives no numeric values. The data is not described in the abstract and is believed to be real connected-vehicle data, which has not been verified (see section 5).
4. **B17 Ben Othmane et al., IEEE TDSC (accepted 2020).**
   - Why it matters. Real in-motion vehicle (Ford Transit 500 2017). Injected speed and RPM messages test the R5-type relation (speed vs RPM), comparing a simple physical correlation with unsupervised ML (k-means, HMM). This is directly relevant to RQ3, and it warns that off-vehicle test benches can mis-evaluate detectors.
   - Key findings (abstract): "the Pearson correlation distinguishes the two states, the k-means clustering method has an acceptable accuracy but high false positive rate and HMM ... has a high false positive". The authors recommend integrating "knowledge about how the ECUs collaborate".
5. **B13 Digital Twin-Based IDS for Powertrain CAN: Rahman, Salek & Chowdhury, arXiv 2026 (PREPRINT).**
   - Why it matters. Almost the same setting as ours: decoded CAN signals of a real Hyundai/Kia car (17 signals, including two categorical gear signals), residual-threshold detection, and plateau / drift / masquerade / gear-masquerade attacks. It reports high false-positive rates, which shows why the false-alarm budget matters. It is relevant to RQ1 and RQ4 and is the most recent competitor. Its physics is learned (a DT), not explicit equations, so a D1 with explicit least-squares relations is a clear point of difference.
   - Key numbers (abstract): "detection rates of 94.6% for continuous drift and 89.2% for masquerade, while the baseline detected almost none of the fabricated payload attacks"; "False positive rates reached 39.6%".
6. **B01 Ganesan, Rao & Shin, SAE 2017 (project source [5]), flagged seminal; pair it with B05 EVAD (IEEE TVT 2019, 52 cites).**
   - Why they matter. Together they establish "natural redundancy" / cross-sensor consistency on real vehicle data: IVBSS naturalistic data in B01, and the Udacity "Open Sourcing 223GB of Driving Data" logs in B05. They give empirical support for R1 (wheel speeds), R2 (wheel-speed derivative vs acceleration), R5 (RPM vs speed) and R6 (steering vs yaw). In both, the relations are learned correlations rather than fitted physical equations, which is the gap D1 fills.
   - Key numbers: B05 (abstract): "average true positive rate achieves 99.5% with 1% false positive rate". B01 (abstract): "we use the pairwise correlation between key variables, and use cluster-analysis to identify distinct behavior of drivers", with only "preliminary results".
