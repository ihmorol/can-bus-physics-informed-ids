# D. CAN IDS datasets, benchmarks, evaluation methodology and surveys: search log, screening and shortlist

Prepared 2026-11-23. Scope: (a) CAN IDS datasets/benchmarks with signal-level data and critiques of dataset realism; (b) evaluation methodology for CAN/automotive IDS; (c) high-impact surveys/SLRs 2019-2026; (d) status of existing sources [2], [11], [12].

Screening rules applied: I1 peer-reviewed (preprints only if exceptional, flagged), I2 2019-2026 (older = "seminal", threat background only), I3 dataset / benchmark / evaluation-methodology / survey for automotive in-vehicle network IDS. E1 = individual detector paper with no dataset or methodology contribution. E2 = non-automotive dataset or methodology.

All decisions below were made on the abstract (or, where stated, on full text). Citation counts are OpenAlex `cited_by_count` read via api.openalex.org on 2026-11-23 unless marked "S2" (Semantic Scholar Graph API, same date). The two sources give different numbers for the same paper (e.g., X-CANIDS: OpenAlex 89, S2 68), so treat them as rough indicators only. Scopus status is a venue-level judgement only: **no Scopus query was run**.

## 3. Shortlist for this project (ranked)

### 1. D13 - Jeong et al., X-CANIDS (IEEE TVT) + X-CANIDS dataset (IEEE DataPort) - DATASET JUSTIFICATION
- Why it matters: it is the dataset source, and it is the only public real-vehicle dataset screened whose signals are decoded with a public, named DBC. That is what makes it possible to write the physics equations (wheel speeds, yaw rate, longitudinal and lateral acceleration, rpm).
- Abstract (locator "abstract"): "X-CANIDS dissects the payloads in CAN messages into human-understandable signals using a CAN database." / "The signals improve the intrusion detection performance compared with the use of bit representations of raw payloads." / "X-CANIDS can detect zero-day attacks because it does not require any labeled dataset in the training phase." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/tvt.2023.3327275)
- Dataset page facts (locator "IEEE DataPort record"): "The dataset contains CAN messages from Hyundai LF Sonata 2017 e-VGT."; "Using hyundai_ccan_2015.dbc (kindly visit https://github.com/commaai/opendbc/blob/master/hyundai_2015_ccan.dbc), you could obtain 688 signals."; "Each dataset contains 688 signal columns and one label column."; benign set = "Six driving dataset + one idling dataset"; the intrusion set is built from Dump 6, with attacks in "the period 480-1440 s"; attacks are fuzzing, fabrication, suspension, masquerade, replay; licence CC BY 4.0. — [IEEE DataPort, DOI 10.21227/epsj-y384](https://ieee-dataport.org/node/12755)
- Implication for the split: seven separate recordings exist, so splitting by whole recording is feasible. Because every built-in attack sits in one dump (Dump 6), held-out attack testing cannot be spread across recordings unless the project injects its own attacks. This supports the planned custom offset/drift/freeze/replay injections.

### 2. D01 - Verma et al., ROAD dataset guide (PLOS ONE 2024) - DATASET CRITIQUE AND WHY NOT ROAD
- Abstract: "State-of-the-art public CAN IDS datasets are limited to real fabrication (simple message injection) attacks and simulated attacks often in synthetic data, lacking fidelity." / "In general, the physical effects of attacks on the vehicle are not verified in the available datasets." / "Only one dataset provides signal-translated data but is missing a corresponding 'raw' binary version." / "...usually with attacks that are too easily detectable to truly test the method" / "The scarcity of appropriate data has stymied comparability and reproducibility of results for researchers." / ROAD has "over 3.5 hours of one vehicle's CAN data". — [PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0296879)
- Full-text facts on ROAD's signals (locator: Sec. 4, 4.2 "Obfuscation", Appendix 6.3):
  - The vehicle make and model are not disclosed ("the make/model of which our organization will not allow us to disclose").
  - "IDs have been anonymized in such a way that arbitration order/priority is not preserved".
  - "Data fields have been scrambled in such a way that signals have been preserved".
  - Signals come from reverse engineering: "We used CAN-D [61] to translate the signals and generate DBCs, using the heuristic signal boundary classifier".
  - Only "17 of the 33 attack captures and the all ambient captures" are signal-translated.
  - Attacks were "physically verified and documented" on a dynamometer.
  - The "Correlated Signal" attack injects four mutually inconsistent wheel speeds into "the single ID communicating the four wheels' speeds".
  - Sec. 4.3 lists the drawbacks: dynamometer-only attacks, a single vehicle, 100 μs timestamps, interval (not per-message) labels.
- Inference: ROAD's signals come from an algorithm-generated DBC with anonymised IDs and an undisclosed vehicle. There are no OEM signal names or confirmed units, so physical equations cannot be written from documented semantics. This is my reading of the full text, not a sentence in the paper. The ROAD files themselves were not inspected. The Correlated Signal attack is an example of a physically inconsistent attack that cross-wheel consistency checks target.

### 3. D07 - Lazeski et al., "WIP: On the Real-World Applicability of Automotive CAN IDS" (USENIX VehicleSec '26) - EVALUATION CRITIQUE AND MOTIVATION FOR DOMAIN KNOWLEDGE
- Abstract: "We observe that many commonly used CAN IDS datasets only include simple and easily distinguishable attacks." / "ML-based models trained on these datasets tend to learn dataset-specific statistical artifacts or shortcuts rather than robust or semantically meaningful detection logic" / "high detection performance is not inherently indicative of practical robustness." / "new detection strategies founded upon vehicle specifications and domain knowledge, in combination with ML tools, may offer more transparent and reliable protection than purely ML-driven approaches". — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/vehiclesec26/presentation/lazeski)
- Caveat: it is a work-in-progress paper, and its findings are limited to "per-frame, binary intrusion detection" (abstract). It directly supports (a) not relying on the built-in, trivially detectable X-CANIDS attacks and (b) the research hypothesis that domain knowledge plus ML is better than ML alone.

### 4. D08 - Sharmin et al., benchmarking review (J. Computer Security 2024) + D06 - Koltai et al. (IEEE EuroS&PW 2026) - EVALUATION COMPARABILITY
- D08 abstract: "These CAN IDS are evaluated in disparate experimental conditions that vary in terms of the workload used, the features used, the metrics reported, etc., which makes direct comparison difficult." Its design space has five aspects: "IDS type, attack model, evaluation type, workload generation, and evaluation metrics". — [JCS](https://doi.org/10.3233/jcs-230027)
- D06 abstract: "the evaluation of CAN IDS methods remains difficult due to inconsistencies in experimental setups and the lack of standardized benchmarking frameworks. As a result, reported performance often depends on dataset-specific characteristics" / the authors "integrate seven publicly available CAN IDS datasets" and evaluate "five conceptually different IDS approaches", showing "detection performance can vary significantly across datasets". — [EuroS&PW](https://doi.org/10.1109/eurospw72509.2026.00048)
- Use: justifies a fixed, pre-registered protocol (one frozen threshold per detector, identical attack sets, multiple seeds) so that physics-augmented and data-only detectors are compared on equal footing. D08 gives the vocabulary for describing that protocol.

### 5. D05 - Lampe, Kidmose & Meng, can-sleuth (IJIS 2025) - TRAIN/TEST SPLITTING
- Abstract: "we investigate the impact of train-test interdependence in the three UNIMORE datasets" / can-train-and-test's "testing scenarios can reveal when an IDS has overfitted to a particular vehicle type". — [IJIS](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10207-025-01038-8)
- Companion D02 abstract: "researchers can select a subset of the attacks for training and save the remainder for testing". — [C&S](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2024.103777)
- Use: the closest peer-reviewed automotive support found for treating train-test dependence as a validity threat. It supports splitting by whole recording. Caveat: the abstract does not say how the dependence arises, and the full text was not checked.

### 6. D17 - Rajapaksha et al. (ACM Computing Surveys 2023) + D26 - Loukas et al. (Ad Hoc Networks 2019) - SURVEY FRAMING
- D17 abstract: "This article surveys AI-based in-vehicle IDS from 2016 to 2022 (August) with a novel taxonomy. It reviews the detection techniques, attack types, features, and benchmark datasets." It also "identifies the limitations of existing proposals". 317 citations (OpenAlex, 2026-11-23), the highest-cited survey from 2020 onward found. — [ACM CSUR](https://doi.org/10.1145/3570954)
- D26 abstract: "Movement, as well as other physical manifestations of their operation may allow cyber security breaches to lead to physical damage, but can also be an opportunity for detection. For example, physical sensing can contribute to more accurate or more rapid intrusion detection through observation and analysis of physical manifestations of a security breach." / "attacks against vehicles are very rare, often making knowledge-based intrusion detection systems less practical than behaviour-based ones". — [Ad Hoc Networks](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adhoc.2018.10.002)
- Use: D17 is the authoritative recent AI-IDS survey. D26 is the survey anchor for the cyber-physical/physics-aware idea. Alternatives for recency: D24 El-Fatyany et al., IEEE COMST 2026 (covers 2020-2025), and D18 Lampe & Meng, IEEE COMST 2023.

Seminal threat background (not ranked): D28 Koscher et al. 2010 and D29 Checkoway et al. 2011 (see Section 4).

## 5. Open uncertainties

1. **Threshold and false-alarms-per-hour rules lack an automotive-specific peer-reviewed source in this search.** None of the screened abstracts explicitly prescribes setting a threshold at a high percentile of attack-free validation scores, or reporting false alarms per hour. These are defensible design choices, but this search does not back them with a citation. Candidates to check in full text: D08 (its "evaluation metrics" dimension), D23 (it reviews "evaluation metrics ... to meet the requirements of safety-critical systems"), D16 (online streaming evaluation), and the non-automotive Urbina et al. (D35; its CCS 2016 peer-reviewed version was not screened), which proposes a false-alarm vs attack-impact trade-off metric.
2. **Data leakage / splitting by recording.** The only automotive evidence found is D05's "train-test interdependence" analysis (abstract level). Query O18 found no CAN-specific leakage paper. General-NIDS critiques exist (Engelen et al. 2021; Apruzzese et al. 2022), but they are E2 and were not screened on abstract.
3. **ROAD signal naming.** "ROAD signals are unnamed/unsemantic" is inferred from the ROAD paper's full text (algorithm-generated DBC via CAN-D, anonymised IDs, undisclosed vehicle). The released files were not opened to confirm column names.
4. **X-CANIDS publication year.** OpenAlex and S2 both list 2023 (DOI 10.1109/TVT.2023.3327275 indicates 2023 early access). The project cites 2024, probably the issue year. The volume, issue and pages were not checked.
5. **The built-in X-CANIDS attacks are "trivially detectable".** This is the project's own observation. D07 and D01 support it in general terms for common datasets, but neither tests X-CANIDS specifically. D32 (CANival) reports results on the X-CANIDS dataset (abstract: average TPR 0.960, TNR 0.997), which is consistent with high detectability but not proof of it.
6. **D07 is a WIP paper,** and its USENIX page lists a fifth author (Wiesmaier) who is absent from the BibTeX and the athene-center listing. Confirm the author list from the proceedings PDF (the PDF could not be text-extracted here).
7. **Citation counts** differ between OpenAlex and S2, so state the source with each number. Scopus indexing was judged at venue level only; VehicleSec (NDSS/USENIX), USENIX Security and the EICC proceedings are marked "unknown".
8. **Checkoway et al. 2011 abstract.** The fetch tool returned it summarised plus one verbatim phrase. Re-read it on the USENIX page before quoting more.
9. **Not covered:** CAN-D (Verma et al., IEEE TVT, the signal reverse-engineering method behind ROAD), the HCRL Survival-Analysis and OTIDS dataset papers, and the TUdatalib dataset from D07. None were screened. Add them if the dataset table needs completeness.
