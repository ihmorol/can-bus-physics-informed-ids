## 3. Shortlist (most useful for this project) and answers to the key questions

### 3.1 Ranked shortlist

**1. E08: Erba et al., "Constrained Concealment Attacks against Reconstruction-based Anomaly Detectors in Industrial Control Systems", ACSAC 2020, https://doi.org/10.1145/3427228.3427660**
- Why it matters for RQ4 / consistent-group: this is the best empirical precedent for the "consistent group" attacker. It shows that learned correlation (physics-proxy) detectors catch partial tampering because cross-signal correlations break. It also shows that an attacker who changes a subset of signals consistently with learned physical constraints evades them. This directly justifies the consistent-group attack as a known threat class, and it explains the expected result that partial (single-signal) tampering is caught while consistent tampering is not.
- Key facts (abstract):
  - "replay attacks ... show bad performance (i.e., increasing the number of alarms) if the attacker is constrained to manipulate less than 95% of all features in the system, as hidden correlations between the features are not replicated well" (abstract).
  - "we propose two novel attacks that manipulate a subset of the sensor readings, leveraging learned physical constraints of the system" (abstract).
  - "the detector's Recall drops from 0.68 to 0.12 by manipulating 4 sensors out of 82 in WADI dataset" (abstract); black-box attacks "work against autoencoder-, LSTM-, and CNN-based detectors" (abstract).

**2. E01: Urbina et al., "Limiting the Impact of Stealthy Attacks on Industrial Control Systems", ACM CCS 2016, https://doi.org/10.1145/2976749.2978388 (SEMINAL)**
- Why it matters: it is the canonical security-venue statement that physics-based detectors are evaluated wrongly unless the attacker is stealthy, adaptive and stays close to expected behaviour. It also ties evaluation to a false-alarm upper bound, which matches the project's "same false-alarm budget" design. Use it to justify the evaluation methodology for RQ4 and the physics-consistent attacker.
- Key facts (abstract):
  - "Attackers that want to remain undetected can attempt to hide their manipulation of the system by following closely the expected behavior of the system, while injecting just enough false information at each time step to achieve their goals" (abstract).
  - "many of those works use detection schemes that are not limiting the impact of stealthy attacks" (abstract).
  - "We propose a new metric to measure the impact of stealthy attacks and how they relate to our selection on an upper bound on false alarms" (abstract).

**3. E16: Longari et al., "Assessing the Resilience of Automotive Intrusion Detection Systems to Adversarial Manipulation", ACM TCPS 2025, https://doi.org/10.1145/3737294 (with E15, VehicleSec 2024, as the earlier conference version)**
- Why it matters: this is the most complete recent peer-reviewed study of adaptive attackers against CAN IDS. It also checks whether evasive perturbations keep the attack's physical effect, which is the trade-off behind the consistent-group attack (stealth vs impact). It covers attacker knowledge levels comparable to the project's threat model (the attacker does not know thresholds, which is closest to grey/black-box).
- Key facts (abstract):
  - "We consider three scenarios: white-box ..., grey-box ..., and the more realistic black-box (no knowledge of the IDS' internal workings or data)" (abstract).
  - "we study effect of the adversarial perturbation on the attack impact and evaluate real-time feasibility by precomputing evasive payloads for timed injection based on bus traffic" (abstract).
  - "their effectiveness is strongly dependent on the dataset quality, the target IDS, and the attacker's degree of knowledge" (abstract).
  - Caveat: these attacks are gradient-based payload perturbations, not physics-consistent signal edits. A web-search summary of the arXiv version says perturbations sometimes made "the payload similar to a non-tampered one". That claim was not verified in the abstract.

**4. E10 (+E11): Dash et al., "Out of control: stealthy attacks against robotic vehicles protected by control-based techniques", ACSAC 2019, https://doi.org/10.1145/3359789.3359847**
- Why it matters: this is the vehicle-domain proof that control/physics-model IDS (residual against an estimation model) can be evaded by stealthy attackers who stay within detector tolerance, with real impact. It supports the claim in RQ4 that physics checks have a blind region.
- Key facts (abstract): "we propose three kinds of attacks to evade the control-based detection techniques and cause RVs to malfunction"; the attacks need no "specific details of the RV"; "the control models are incapable of detecting our stealthy attacks, and ... the attacks can have significant adverse impact on the RV's mission (e.g., cause the RV to crash or deviate from its target significantly)" (abstract).

**5. E02 / E04 (theory, pick one or both): Pasqualetti et al., IEEE TAC 2013, https://doi.org/10.1109/tac.2013.2266831; Mo & Sinopoli, IEEE TAC, https://doi.org/10.1109/tac.2015.2498708 (SEMINAL)**
- Why it matters: these give the theoretical reason residual/physics detectors miss consistent attacks.
  - Pasqualetti: monitoring has "fundamental monitoring limitations from system-theoretic and graph-theoretic perspectives" (abstract). Not verified (full text not read): the paper's well-known undetectability condition is that attacked outputs coincide with outputs from some other admissible state or initial condition. This matches the consistent-group attack, which makes the forged signals look like a different but physically valid driving state.
  - Mo & Sinopoli: the attacker compromises "a subset of the sensors and injecting an exogenous control input, without incurring detection from an anomaly detector", and "the characterization of the maximum perturbation can be posed as reachable set computation" (abstract).
- Optional add-on: E03, Teixeira et al., Automatica 2015, for the attacker resource taxonomy ("An attack space defined by the adversary's system knowledge, disclosure, and disruption resources"; covers "denial-of-service, replay, zero-dynamics, and bias injection attacks", abstract, arXiv version). It can be used to place the project's masquerade attacker (disruption = silence ECU + inject; knowledge = signal semantics; no detector-threshold knowledge) in an established framework.

Runners-up (cite as support, not core):
- E09, Erba & Tippenhauer ACSAC 2022: learned detectors "verify a combination of temporal, spatial, and statistical consistencies"; "generic concealment attacks are designed to violate a specific consistency verified by the detector, and require no knowledge of the attacked physical process or the detector" (abstract). This is a good template for designing RQ4 attacks per consistency type.
- E14, Bhatia et al. NDSS 2021 (DUET): shows that masquerade on real cars is a studied threat. "the CAN is susceptible to the ECU masquerade attack in which a compromised (attacker) ECU impersonates an uncompromised (victim) ECU and spoofs the latter's CAN messages"; DUET reaches "an impersonation success rate of at least 90% in evading two state-of-the-art VIDS" (abstract).
- E18, Moriano et al. AutoSec 2022: "To overcome time-based detection methods, adversaries must administer masquerade attacks by sending frames in lieu of (and therefore at the expected time of) benign frames but with malicious payloads"; "masquerade attacks modify the nuanced correlations of CAN signal time series" (abstract). This justifies the normal-timing masquerade threat model and correlation-based detection.
- E13, Khazraei et al. CDC 2024: "the attacker can cause arbitrarily large deviation in the position trajectory by compromising only the GPS measurements", and "the attacker does not need to have access to the model of the system" (abstract). Vehicle sensor-fusion stealth.
- E25, AVMON VehicleSec 2024: physics-model defences "can be limited by the accuracy of this prediction which can leave room for an attacker to operate without being detected" (abstract). This states the noise/tolerance blind spot of physics residuals.

### 3.2 Answers to the key questions

**Q1. Seminal works on stealthy/undetectable attacks against residual detectors (2-3, venues verified).**
- Takeaway: the three to cite are Pasqualetti, Dörfler & Bullo (IEEE TAC 2013, theory of undetectable attacks), Teixeira et al. (Automatica vol. 51, pp. 135-148, Jan 2015, zero-dynamics and bias attacks, resource taxonomy) and Urbina et al. (ACM CCS 2016, stealthy attacks vs physics detectors with false-alarm-bounded evaluation). Mo & Sinopoli (IEEE TAC technical note) is the χ²-detector alternative.
- Venues and years were checked against OpenAlex and Crossref (Automatica metadata from the Crossref API). The Mo & Sinopoli year is 2015 in OpenAlex. Its journal issue year was not verified and it is often cited as 2016.

**Q2. Peer-reviewed 2019-2026 papers that attack or evade in-vehicle CAN IDS.**

### 3.3 Inferences for RQ4 (agent's own reasoning, not from sources)
- The consistent-group attack (scale every related signal by the same factor k) maps to a forged trajectory that is a different but physically admissible state (the car "driving at k·v"). In the Pasqualetti/Teixeira framework this is an undetectable or zero-residual attack for any detector that checks only those relations. So physics features should give no gain on it by construction. That is a principled negative result, not a weakness.
- Uniform scaling does not keep every relation in the project's list, because they are not all homogeneous of degree 1:
  - Linear relations still hold: wheel speeds vs vehicle speed, d(speed)/dt vs longitudinal acceleration (if acceleration is also scaled by k), and rpm/speed within gear (if rpm is also scaled).
  - Yaw rate vs rear-wheel speed difference scales yaw by k.
  - Lateral acceleration = speed × yaw rate is bilinear. If speed and yaw are both scaled by k, consistency needs lateral acceleration scaled by k², not k.
  - So a "same factor" attack across all groups is inconsistent with the bilinear relation, unless yaw/lateral signals are left out of the attacked group. The paper should state exactly which signals the consistent group contains. This is the agent's derivation and should be checked.
- Erba 2020's "<95% of features" finding suggests the "whole message" attack (all four wheel speeds edited together) will still be caught if the other ECUs' speed sources and the longitudinal accelerometer are not edited. Physics features should help there. This is consistent with E18's claim that masquerade breaks inter-signal correlations.
